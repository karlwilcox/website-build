This is a good companion piece to the [Apollo Guidance Computer](/reviews/books/non-fiction/apollo-guidance-computer/)
work. That book covered the internal workings of the AGC, whereas this one looked much more
at the human factors. It was interesting to read about the initial research into questions of
just exactly _*how*_ humans should interact with automation, what sort of interface should be
provided and what was the split of responsibilities; and the inevitable conflicts between
the "test pilots" and the "engineers".

I liked the detailed breakdowns of the moon landings, they really did provide some insights
into how man and computer worked together. It was also hinted at (but never really
explained) that the flexibility of the AGC played a major role in saving the Apollo 13
crew but to be fair the author did suggest that was covered adequately elsewhere. Shame
though, it would have made this a more complete account.

I was somewhat surprised on reaching the end as I had barely passed the halfway mark of
the page count! There is an enormously comprehensive set of notes, bibliography and
index putting this more as a scholarly work rather than a popular account, and
probably better for it for a detail nerd like me!

Recommended for anyone interested in the history of space flight and also for
its insights into how the aircraft and spacecraft of today work as well.
